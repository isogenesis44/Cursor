I want to start with a number that made me put my phone down for a second when I first read it. Researchers tracked lottery winners for years after their win, and on average, within about a year, their reported happiness had settled back to roughly where it was before they won. Not lower. Not higher in any lasting way. Roughly the same. Somebody wins eighty million dollars and twelve months later they're about as satisfied with their Tuesday as they were the Tuesday before the ticket. That's the hedonic treadmill in one sentence, and today I want to actually walk you through what it feels like at every rung of the ladder, because most people talk about this concept in the abstract, and I think the abstract version misses what it's actually like to live inside it.

Let's start at the bottom rung, because it matters, and because this is the one place where the treadmill genuinely doesn't apply the same way. If you're living below a certain threshold — can't reliably pay rent, choosing between the electric bill and groceries some months — more money does produce more happiness, pretty reliably, pretty linearly. Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton did the famous study on this back in 2010, and even though later research complicated their exact dollar figure, the core finding held up: financial stress is a genuine, physical drain on wellbeing, and relieving it produces a real, sustained lift. I bring this up first because I don't want anyone walking away from this video thinking money is some kind of illusion that never helps. For a huge number of people, it helps enormously, and pretending otherwise is its own kind of dishonesty.

Now here's where it gets interesting — the point where you've covered the basics. Rent's handled. Food's handled. You're not lying awake doing math on your phone at 2am. This is where a lot of research, including a big 2021 study out of Purdue, found something people don't expect: happiness kept climbing with income for a while, then the curve started to flatten, hovering somewhere in the neighborhood of ninety to a hundred thousand dollars a year depending on the country and cost of living. Above that line, more money kept adding small amounts of life satisfaction, but the emotional day-to-day stuff — how good your Tuesday actually feels — basically stopped moving.

I think the clearest way to picture this stage is a story a friend told me about buying his first proper mattress. He'd slept on a secondhand futon for six years, and when he finally bought a real bed, he said the first two weeks were genuinely euphoric. Falling asleep felt like a treat. And then, without him doing anything wrong, without the mattress changing at all, it just became... the bed. Not disappointing. Just normal. That's basically the whole mechanism in miniature. Your brain isn't built to register a static improvement as ongoing joy. It's built to register change, and once the change stops, so does the emotional signal, even though the improvement is still very much there.

This is the part where I need to explain the actual biology, because I think a lot of people hear "hedonic treadmill" and assume it's some kind of vague psychological theory. It's not. It's tied to dopamine, and dopamine's job in your brain isn't really to reward you for having things — it's to reward you for the anticipation of getting things, and then it drops right back down once you've got them. Wolfram Schultz's research on this, going back to the nineties with monkeys and juice rewards, showed that dopamine neurons fire hardest at the moment of prediction and surprise, not at the moment of consumption. So when you buy the new car, the peak isn't really driving it. The peak was the week before, imagining driving it. Once it's sitting in your driveway every day, your brain has already filed it under "normal life," and it stops paying out.

Let's go up a rung — genuinely comfortable. Six figures, paid-off car, maybe a house, no real financial stress but not lavish either. And here's a strange thing that happens at this level that almost nobody talks about: the treadmill doesn't just apply to purchases anymore. It applies to comparison. You stop comparing yourself to where you used to be and start comparing yourself to your actual peer group, and your peer group has shifted upward along with you. There's a concept economists call the reference group effect, and it's almost comically consistent: a person earning ninety thousand dollars surrounded by people earning sixty thousand generally reports feeling wealthier and more satisfied than a person earning a hundred and twenty thousand surrounded by people earning two hundred thousand. Same treadmill. Different scenery. You're not running toward an absolute number anymore. You're running to stay level with whoever's next to you, and the person next to you keeps changing as your own life changes.

I remember reading about a neighborhood in California — Marin County — where I recall a study describing residents earning close to two hundred thousand dollars a year who genuinely felt financially strained, not performing it, actually feeling it, because their neighbors were doctors and tech executives clearing seven figures. The number on their paycheck was, by almost any national standard, enormous. The feeling in their gut was scarcity. That gap between the actual number and the felt experience is the whole treadmill, just wearing a nicer outfit than the lottery winner story.

Now let's go further up — real wealth, the kind where basic comparison to neighbors stops making sense because you don't really have neighbors in the traditional sense anymore. This is where something shifts that I think is genuinely counterintuitive: the treadmill doesn't slow down at this level. If anything, it speeds up, because the size of the "next thing" required to produce a dopamine spike has to keep growing to register at all. A modest vacation used to feel like an event. Now it takes something enormous to produce that same flicker — a private trip somewhere genuinely rare, an experience nobody else you know has had. I read an account from someone who'd made real money in tech describing how a five-star hotel stopped registering as special after maybe the tenth one. Not because he was ungrateful. Because his brain had quietly moved the goalposts, the exact same way it moved them when the futon became the mattress became the bed. The scale changed completely. The mechanism didn't change at all.

There's an analogy I keep coming back to for this whole thing, and it's simpler than any of the economics. Think of your happiness baseline like a thermostat set to a specific temperature. Windfall money is like opening a window on a cold day — it warms the room up fast, feels great immediately, and then the thermostat notices the change and quietly works to bring the room back down to its set point. It's not sabotage. It's just what thermostats do. The house doesn't stay warm because the window let in one gust of heat. It stays warm if you change the actual setting, which is a much slower, much harder thing to do than opening a window.

So what does actually move the thermostat's setting, if buying things doesn't? This is genuinely the most useful part of the research, so stay with me here. Sonja Lyubomirsky's work on this breaks life satisfaction into rough categories — a slice that's just genetic temperament, a slice that's circumstances like income and location, and a surprisingly large slice that's intentional activity — the stuff you actually choose to do with your days. And the interesting finding is that circumstances, including money, are one of the smaller slices, while intentional activity is one of the biggest. Meaning: what actually raises the thermostat setting isn't what you have. It's what you regularly do — relationships you invest in, skills you're building, meaning you're actively constructing, not meaning you're passively hoping a purchase will hand you.

I think about a story a hospice nurse told once, about the regrets she heard most often from people near the end. Almost nobody talked about the car they didn't buy or the house that wasn't big enough. People talked about relationships they let go cold, or work they never got around to that actually mattered to them. That's not a coincidence. That's the treadmill from the other end of the telescope — proof, in the saddest possible dataset, of exactly what the Lyubomirsky research predicts. The stuff that doesn't habituate is connection and meaning. The stuff that does habituate, almost without exception, is possessions and status.

Here's something I think gets skipped in almost every version of this conversation: the treadmill isn't just about pleasure going away. It's also, somewhat mercifully, about pain fading the same way. Philip Brickman's original 1978 study — the one that basically started this whole field — didn't just look at lottery winners. He also looked at people who'd become paraplegic after accidents, and found that after the initial devastating period, their reported day-to-day happiness climbed back toward, though not always fully to, their pre-accident baseline. The same mechanism that steals the joy from your new car is the same mechanism that eventually gives some of the joy back after genuine tragedy. It's not a cruel trick. It's actually one of the more humane things your brain does for you, even though it costs you the lasting thrill of good news too.

So where does that leave you, practically, whatever rung you're actually standing on right now. I think the honest answer is: stop expecting the next purchase, the next raise, the next milestone to be the one that finally sticks. It won't, not because you're doing something wrong, but because nobody's does. The mattress becomes the bed. The bed becomes the house. The house becomes the neighborhood you're now comparing yourself inside of. What actually holds — what genuinely resists the treadmill — is the stuff you build on purpose and keep tending to on purpose: the friendships you actually maintain, the skill you're actually improving, the version of your days that you'd still choose even if nobody was watching and nothing new was arriving in the mail.

I'll leave you with this. Somebody earning thirty thousand dollars and somebody earning thirty million dollars are, in a very real sense, running on the exact same machine, at the exact same effort level, just with wildly different scenery scrolling past on the screen in front of them. The machine doesn't care about the number. It only cares about the next step.
